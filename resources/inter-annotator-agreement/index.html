Inter-annotator agreement, explained without the math anxiety

What agreement metrics tell you, what they hide, and how to use them to improve data instead of just reporting a number.

Why agreement matters

If independent people label the same item differently, the model learns noise. Agreement measures how often qualified annotators reach the same answer. Low agreement usually means the task or the guideline is unclear, not that people are careless.

Raw agreement versus chance-corrected scores

Raw percent agreement is easy to read but flatters tasks where one label dominates. Chance-corrected measures such as Cohen's kappa (two raters) or Krippendorff's alpha (many raters, missing data) adjust for agreement that would happen by luck. Use raw agreement for intuition and a corrected score for decisions.

Look per label, not just overall

A single overall score can hide one label that nobody agrees on. Break agreement down by label, by locale and by annotator. The weakest slice is where your next guideline fix should go.

Agreement is not accuracy

A team can agree consistently on the wrong answer. Pair agreement with hidden reference items that have verified answers, so you measure both consistency and correctness.

Turn the number into action

Every agreement report should end with changes: rules added to the guideline, examples updated, annotators recalibrated. A metric that does not change the work is decoration.
